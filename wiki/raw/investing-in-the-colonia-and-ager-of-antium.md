INVESTING IN THE COLONIA
AND AGER OF ANTIUM
Tymon de Haas · Gijs Tol · Peter Attema
Introduction
Archaeologicalsurveys in central Italy invariably indicate a peak in the number
of rural settlement sites during the Late Republican and Early Imperial period,
which is simultaneous with the urban expansion of Rome and surrounding central
Italian towns. It is generally assumed that this peak in rural settlement and urban ex-
pansion indicates a substantially growing rural and urban population.1
A central question in the current debate on the ancient economy is whether this
population growth went hand-in-hand with aggregate economic growth only, or al-
so led to an increase in per capitaincomes. In a recent article, Wim Jongman succinctly
described the problem: ‘[…] was population growth the product of rising prosperity,
and was population growth in and of itself a further stimulus to the economy, or was
it at the expense of standard of living (there were more people to share the same
pie/land)?’2
In this paper, we will explore the use of a variety of archaeological indicators in or-
der to evaluate whether aggregate economic growth occurred in the Late Republican
and Early Imperial period and whether this also led to an increase in standard of liv-
ing for both elite and non-elite segments of society. For our analysis, we use the cor-
pus of rural settlement data collected for the Carta Archeologica di Nettuno, a study that
covers part of the ager of the Roman colonyAntium (Fig. 1).3
In the Late Republican and Early Imperial period, Rome and its surroundings were
integrated into a single socio-economic system, often referred to as the Suburbium,
and the case study area would certainly have fallen within it.4This paper therefore al-
so evaluates to what extent the economic developments in our study area should be
seen the wider context of the Suburbium.
It should be remarked at the outset that this paper presents the first result of an
analysis of this kind, and, as such, forms the basis for further work based on newly
acquired data from the ager of Antium, as well as from the Pontine region as a whole.5

Economic growth and archaeological data:
aim and method of the study
In a recent study, Bowman and Wilson indicate three possible approaches to an ar-
chaeological study of economic growth:
1) estimation of GDP (Gross Domestic Product);
2) identification of possible driversof growth (including trade, intensification of cap-
ital investment, improved technology, institutional attitudes and stimuli, and in-
creased labour division);
3) identification of possible reflectionsof growth (for example urbanization, import
replacement, increased consumption and improved standard of living).1
1 Bowman- Wilson2009, pp. 12-13; see also http://www.romaneconomy.ox.ac.uk/.

Survey data in our view have the potential to look at both driversand reflectionsof
growth.
The main aim of this study is therefore to develop methods by which we can use
archaeological (survey) data to assess whether population growth went hand-in-hand
with economic growth and if so, whether this entailed aggregategrowth only, or also
implied per capitagrowth.
We are fully aware that per capitagrowth in a strict sense requires detailed knowl-
edge of population size and the size of economic output – both of which are not with-
in the reach of archaeology. As per capitagrowth would, in our view, imply that both
elites and non-elites benefit from growth, the matter could be simplified by looking
at evidence for per capita growth in both these groups. We therefore propose to study
the material culture of different rural site classes connecting it to elites (villae mariti-
maeand villae) and non-elites (farms and villages).1
An additional aim of this paper is to study the historical and geographical back-
ground of growth in our study area. In our view, Late Republican and Early Imperi-
al growth cannot be understood without knowledge of long term developments in
settlement and demography on the one hand and an understanding of the role of
Rome and the Suburbium in these developments on the other hand. With regard to
the latter, we will also look at direct involvement of the imperial court, as well as
Antium’s role within the trade networks of the Suburbium.
To provide long-term historical background, we will first discuss the trends in set-
tlement in our case study area and the implications these trends have for the demo-
graphic development of the area. After this, we will explore two potential reflections
of growth – consumption of fine wares and imported foodstuffs (as represented by
amphorae). We will not only deal with changes over time in the supply of these items
(i.e., as an indicator of aggregate growth), but also with changes in their occurrence
at different site types (i.e., as an indicator of per capita growth). Next, we will turn to
possible drivers of growth, as represented in the archaeological data, which include
evidence for agricultural intensification, investments in infrastructure and, finally, in-
vestments in building programs. We finally turn to the historical and epigraphic data
that may inform us on who (both in terms of individuals and institutions) were be-
hind these drivers.
The data set
Between 2004 and 2006, a team from the Groningen Institute of Archaeology (gia)
worked on the compilation of the Carta Archeologica di Nettuno– an inventory of ar-
chaeological data for the municipality of Nettuno, a middle sized town on the
Tyrrhenian coast ca. 60 km southeast of Rome.
The Carta Archeologicais part of the Pontine Region Project (prp) that comprises
systematic archaeological sampling of the various landscape units that make up the
Pontine region.2

From an ancient topographical point of view, the study area is part of the agerof
the Roman colony Antium (Fig. 1). The Carta Archeologica di Nettuno has yielded
important new insights in various aspects of the long term settlement history of the
area from the earliest permanent settlements in the middle Bronze Age to the period
of incastellamentoin the medieval period. As such, it allows an investigation of Late
Republican and Early Imperial socio-economic and demographic developments in
the light of long term settlement trends.
For the creation of the Carta Archeologica, several data sources were used, most
importantly:
- A topographic inventory of archaeological sites in the eastern part of the munici-
pality and the adjacent lower valley of the river Astura.1
- A topographic inventory of archaeological sites in the municipality of Nettuno,
complemented by a museum collection containing archaeological artefacts from
these sites.2
- New fieldwork undertaken by the gia, including both revisits to sites mapped by
the two abovementioned studies and systematic field surveys on agricultural
fields.3
This combination of sources, accompanied by contextual data provided by
epigraphic, cartographical and historical sources has resulted in a reconstruction of
the settlement history of the area.
Development of settlement and settlement typology
Fig. 2 shows the distribution of sites for the various periods in which we have subdi-
vided the Roman period. For each period, sites have – on the basis of datable archae-
ological remains (mainly pottery) – been indicated as either possibly or certainly oc-
cupied. All sites have been classified on the basis of their archaeological remains as
either non-settlement, possible settlement or settlement; the settlements have been
subdivided into farms, villae, villae maritimaeand villages.4
Between 350 and 250 BC (Fig. 2A), there are clusters of sites north of Antium and
along the river Astura, south of the former Archaic town of Satricum. As far as the
data allow us to classify these sites, they are all thought to represent modest farms.
We have very few indications for settlement along the coast and in the southern part
of the Astura valley in this period.
In the period 250-100 BC (Fig. 2B), the number of certainly occupied sites rises, es-
pecially near the mouth of the river Astura. From excavations we know that at least
one of the villae near Antium was constructed in this period, and it seems plausible
that more sites of this period should be seen as early villae.5These potential villae are
located along the coast and north of Antium near a road that connects Antium to the
Alban Hills and the via Appia. In the Astura valley most sites represent farms.

Economic growth in the ager of Antium?
The analysis of the settlement data indicates that urban and rural population both had
reached a peak during the Late Republican and Early Imperial period. Antium itself had
grown into a sizeable port town, its coast was dotted with luxurious villae maritimae,
and its agerwas intensively cultivated. Demographic growth was foremost caused by
an increase in the urban population and residents of rural and maritime villae.1
Demographic growth in itself requires aggregate growth of the local economy in
terms of supplying the basic needs of the urban and rural population. Per capita
growth, however, requires more, as it also implies that this larger population would
have had a higher income and/or an increased standard of living. It is here that we
turn to the archaeological reflections of economic growth.
Reflections of economic growth:
fine ware and amphora consumption patterns
To probe the thesis that per capita growth occurred, we explore changes in the con-
sumption of two ceramic groups, fine wares and imported amphorae, on rural sites.
We consider these categories as “yardsticks” of the access people had to higher-value
commodities and that an increased consumption of table wares and imported oil and
wine imply an increase in the standard of living.2

Drivers and reflections of economic growth: elite investments
Although the evidence for per capita growth – or at least for rising consumption lev-
els within non-elite groups – remains inconclusive, investments (that were evidently
made by elites) provide additional evidence for economic growth. These investments
may, in some cases, be taken as drivers of growth and, in other cases, primarily as re-
flections of growth – although in some cases the two cannot be separated so easily.
In the following we will discuss the various investments and their possible role in eco-
nomic growth. By using a rough chronological framework, it is our intention also to
clarify the relationship between the various investments and the consumption pat-
terns discussed above.

Elite investments: a summary
The evidence we have presented here indicates that investments occurred from the
2ndcentury BC onwards, with particularly large-scale investment in three phases.
The first of these phases dates to the late 2ndand 1stcentury BC, during which var-
ious villae were constructed. At the same time, we also have evidence for construc-
tion works based on the presence of a tile workshop, at which amphorae were also
produced. The latter implies that around this time the elite were involved in special-
ized production of wine and olive oil. From epigraphic sources we know that build-
ing activities took place in the town of Antium itself, and we also see investments in
infrastructure, particularly in the paving of the road leading to the via Appia. The es-
tablishment of villae along this road took place soon after, and it must have stimulat-
ed economic growth, as it improved trade connections with the hinterland.
A second phase of investments occurs in the second half of the 1stcentury AD and
early 2ndcentury AD. Building phases, mosaics and a number of tile and fistulastamps

and at surrounding villae. Sulphur deposits were also exploited in this period. The

A third phase of investments dates to the 3rdand 4thcentury AD. In this period, the

The importance of the road is reflected in the construction of villae along it, while
consumption of imported goods increased dramatically after the construction of the
harbours.
Patrons and investors: elites and the Imperial court
Now that we have discussed the archaeological evidence for elite investments, we will
look at the historical and epigraphic sources, as these provide us with the names of
various wealthy and famous families that may have been responsible for these invest-
ments. The Roman upper-class and the Imperial court had particularly large interests
in the area.

Concluding remarks
In this paper we explored the use of archaeological survey data to study two types of
economic growth in the Roman colony of Antium and its ager: aggregate growth and
per capita growth. The archaeological evidence is unequivocal as regards aggregate
growth during the Late Republican and Early Imperial period. This is not only sug-
gested by the notable peak in both the rural and urban population itself, but also by
the increasing amounts of pottery fine wares and amphorae that were consumed on
rural sites, by the investments made in agricultural specialization and public and pri-
vate building programs. While most of these investments should be seen mainly as
reflections of economic growth, infrastructural improvements and the construction
of Antium’s harbour doubtlessly were important drivers for economic growth.
Moving to the more problematic issue of per capita growth (and linked to that a
possible rise in living standards) we studied changes in the consumption of fine wares
and imported amphorae at rural sites, differentiating between sites associated with
elites (villae and villae maritimae) and non-elites (farms). Although the proposed com-
parative approach seems adequate to measure fluctuations in consumption, the quan-
titative basis is at the moment insufficient to conclude that there was an increased
standard of living for non-elite groups. Larger samples than currently available from
a larger number of farms and villae are needed to arrive at such conclusions.
Our study also shows that a combination of archaeological, historical and epi-
graphic sources can complement survey data, if one wants to identify the drivers and
reflections of economic growth. From the data it appeared that the local and Roman
elite from the Late Republican period onwards invested in various aspects of the lo-
cal economy. They were involved in commercial and agricultural enterprises and in
public and private building programs. The combination of data also allowed the iden-
tification of a number of patrons and investors, some of whom were closely con-
nected to the Imperial court.

From the analysis it is also evident that the economy of Antium cannot be consid-
ered in isolation. The involvement of members of the Roman elite in the area sug-
gests that Antium and its ager were indeed integrated into the Roman Suburbium.
This integration had already started at the beginning of the 2nd century BC, but is
especially clear from the mid 1st century AD. Imperial involvement clearly stimu-
lated Antium’s development at that time, especially with the establishment of a vet-
eran colony and the construction of a harbour under Nero. This harbour enhanced
Antium’s strategic position in the regional trading network, as it connected mar-
itime trade routes with roads that could supply a large part of the southern Sub-
urbium.
To conclude, we realise that some of our analyses, especially that on rural con-
sumption, is at the moment still inconclusive, and further work is needed (and, in-
deed, is currently being carried out). Nonetheless, we would like to highlight the dual
importance of case studies based on multiple source materials, such as the one pre-
sented here. On the one hand, they provide us with detailed local studies that may
add to our understanding of the diversity of Roman towns and their functioning in
the Roman Empire. On the other hand, they may be used to test general hypotheses
on important issues, such as economic growth and the standard of living in the Ro-
man world.

Abstract
Detailed settlement data deriving from archaeological surveys are increasingly used as a proxy
for demographic and economic growth and decline. This paper uses data from field surveys
in the agerof the Roman colony Antium (central Italy) to establish the extent and nature of
economic growth during the Late Republican and Early Imperial period. By using additional
archaeological, historical and epigraphic sources, this contribution looks into the factors that
made growth possible.
Following an overview of the settlement history of the area and a discussion of demo-
graphic trends, we investigate to what extent changes in pottery consumption on rural sites,
particularly of fine wares and imported amphorae, may be used as evidence for aggregate
and/or per capita growth.
While increased levels of consumption for the elite are evident, the analysis is inconclu-
sive for non-elite groups and cannot, on the basis of the presently available data, be taken as
evidence for per capita growth. Next, we consider elite investments, as they may be impor-
tant drivers and reflections of economic growth. A study of archaeological remains, ancient
sources and inscriptions on tiles and fistulaeshows that during the Late Republican and Ear-
ly Imperial period elites were actively involved in all kinds of commercial enterprises, as well
as public and private building programs that boosted the local economy. Infrastructural

Lo studio che qui si presenta, in particolare, si avvale dei dati delle prospezioni condotte
