     Profession and Prestige in the Fasti
     Antiates Ministrorum Domus Augustae



     The Fasti Antiates Ministrorum Domus Augustae, a large inscription associated with the imperial
     villa at Antium, is best known for its iteration of the Augustan calendar. In this article, I reassess
     the fasti in their entirety, focusing on their manner of display and social function. I place special
     emphasis on the section of the inscription, largely overlooked, that contains the annual records of
     magistrates who led the voluntary association that commissioned the inscription, a detailed record
     of two decades of local history. The association revealed in these records worked to bestow honors
     on its members, offering them prestige within and beyond the household, and also acted to censure
     them. Because the fasti identify most magistrates by job title, they also provide invaluable infor-
     mation about the villa’s workforce, composed of enslaved people and freedmen; the document pro-
     vides a valuable counterpoint to other testimonia for occupations, largely funerary. Manual laborers
     and domestics appear with greater frequency in the fasti than they do in occupational inscriptions
     from funerary contexts, while administrators, abundant in funerary contexts, form a minority.
     Importantly, the magistrates’ list reveals that the association was a group in which the “sub-clerical
     grades” of imperial slaves and freedmen could accrue prestige and wield authority as readily as
     their overseers. In the community reflected in the fasti, power derived not from job status within a
     workplace hierarchy, but from social standing among peers—acquired and maintained (or lost) over
     decades of service.

     Keywords: Antium, familia Caesaris, imperial household, enslaved people, freedmen, colle-
     gia, voluntary associations, fasti, calendar, lares, ritual, dining, memory sanctions, occupations,
     household labor

                                       INTRODUCTION

      The imperial villa at Antium was a favorite retreat for the emperors. Museums
worldwide feature sculptures found in the environs, suggestive of its luxurious
décor, including the so-called Borghese Gladiator now in the Louvre and the
Maiden of Antium in the Palazzo Massimo.1 Yet, though recent work has greatly
improved our understanding of the site, much of the evidence for the residence is
anecdotal, relayed by ancient authors in passing.2 It seems that there was some kind

of imperial property in Antium from the early Principate. Strabo testifies that in
his day the emperors (Augustus and Tiberius) enjoyed spending time in the town,
the pleasant climate and fertile territory of which had long made it attractive to
Roman elites.3 Suetonius reports that Caligula and Nero were born there.4 Later,
Nero invested significantly in the residence and the local town: he granted Antium
the status of colonia, summoned the Senate there to celebrate his daughter’s birth,
established a base for the praetorian guard, and created a new harbor (in Suetonius’
words, “a most expensive project,” operis sumptuosissimi) by building a mole that
extended about 850 meters into the sea.5 Thereafter, the residence provided both an
escape from life in Rome and a suitable setting for hosting guests and conducting
affairs of state. Trajan wrote several letters establishing policy while in residence;
Hadrian maintained its library.6 The villa remained in use as an imperial property
through the late second century CE, and likely beyond.7 All in all, this varied evi-
dence suggests that the imperial villa at Antium ranked highly in the emperors’
portfolio of properties, but provides little specific detail about how it was used,
organized, or decorated.
      I here consider the imperial residence at Antium from a different vantage point,
asking what an inscription from the site, the Fasti Antiates Ministrorum Domus
Augustae, can tell us about those who worked there. The inscription has received
little scholarly attention since Degrassi’s careful edition, beyond cursory treatment

     I focus in what follows on the section of the inscription that chronicled the
officers. Yet a few initial observations about how the inscription was arranged as
a whole shed light on its role as record and archive. There was originally a prae-
scriptio, beautifully inscribed and written in large letters, though only four remain.
Comparison with the extant preface of the Fasti Magistrorum Vici suggests that this
lost section either served as a dedication or characterized the nature of the informa-
tion presented, identifying the purpose of the voluntary association.29 The calendar
occupies the bottom third of the inscription. As is typical, its text is arranged in
columns, each day annotated in several ways: with a letter, A–H, indicating where
the day fell in the cycle of market days, nundinae; a Roman numeral showing each
day’s relationship to the kalendae, ides, and nones of the month; and an abbrevia-
tion providing information about its status with respect to official civic business.
To the left, in smaller lettering, were notes providing information about holidays
and festivals, broadly similar to other Augustan calendars, though with several
omissions.30 In contrast, the annual lists, located on the upper portion of the stone,
served as a record of the officers elected to the society, ending in 51 CE. Compared
to the praescriptio and calendar, these sections are notable for their variable, and
less professional-looking, lettering. Guidelines are visible for some entries (41 CE,
42 CE, 48 CE, 49 CE), and several hands are apparent across the extant sections.
For each entry, the names of the consuls are inscribed in larger letters with the offi-
cers underneath—listed by name, and, if an enslaved person, by profession. As I
discuss in greater depth below, there are also indications of more complex practices
of recording. Names of officers were added, erased, or erased and subsequently
written over; extra notes, usually regarding fees, also appear next to some entries.

     In summary, the Fasti Antiates Ministrorum Domus Augustae contained two
related but distinct documents, sharing the same stone and place of display, but
reflective of different practices of record-keeping. The calendar was largely static
from the time of its inscription, but updated with new imperial birthdays.31 In con-
trast, the officer list was compiled year by year, and periodically emended. As a
visual display, the inscription was ever changing, primarily blank when first put on
view, evolving to become a compact chronicle of local history. The close relation-

ship of the annual fasti and the calendar suggests that the act of inscribing the offi-
cers’ names was more than a matter of record, and was closely tied to the group’s
other activities, ritual and social—validating each member’s service and, symboli-
cally, reinforcing the bonds of the wider group.32 Thinking about the inscription as
a whole in this way invites us to consider its impact upon its audience, which may
have been wide (possibly extending to the emperor, his family, his guests, local
potentates, and so on), but which certainly included the officers and the group they
represented. The inscription provided information to these viewers across several
time-horizons. In the short term, or day-to-day, the inscription provided informa-
tion about regional markets to those who needed to track the availability of produce
and other foodstuffs;33 on the monthly time horizon, it documented religious obser-
vances and festivals, useful for those coordinating working and non-working days
and implementing plans for the rituals; on the annual level, it acknowledged and
conferred prestige on those persons who were, we presume, responsible for mus-
tering the society’s resources for the middle term activities of the calendar. These
men have received little scrutiny, both as representatives of their association and as
reflective of the composition of the workforce of the imperial residence.34 Yet our
understanding of the calendar on the Fasti Antiates Ministrorum Domus Augustae
is incomplete without due attention to these individuals and the society they repre-
sented; indeed, the calendar itself existed in large measure to support and promote
the society and its activities.

     Before turning to consider the activities this group engaged in, however, it is

             CONCLUSION: FROM CALENDAR TO COMMUNITY

      To conclude, let us bring these different ways of looking at the Fasti Antiates
Ministrorum Domus Augustae into closer conversation, considering the inscrip-
tion as visual symbol, archive, and expression of collective identity. I have advo-
cated for a holistic approach to this notable inscription, one that establishes the
static calendar and ever-evolving fasti as equal components of an engaging visual
display within the meeting hall. It was both informative and decorative—a sym-
bol of authority that functioned on many levels, depending on the perspective of
the viewer. Based on its findspot, associated with a grand building on the villa’s
grounds, a wide range of viewers would have seen the inscription and reacted to
it variously depending on class, status, and perspective. Indeed, different groups
of viewers might have looked at the monument with different eyes. The calendar,
occupying the lower portion of the stone, was cleanly and clearly inscribed in a
style familiar from public monuments and other documents of the same type; the
list of magistrates, above, was less intelligible, its information recorded in different
hands and with a range of additions and erasures that would have required scrutiny.
      Confronted with this document, elite viewers—the emperor, his family, their
guests, local dignitaries—might find their eyes drawn to the calendar, easy to read
and familiar, well known from Rome and other localities. There, they would see
a reflection of the religious life of the capital city, affirming the ritual power of

of the villa’s numerous and dedicated staff, capable of caring for its grounds, its
marvelous treasures, and its visitors; but the group’s members as individuals would

a reflection of their own community, defined by shared place and experience of
work. Deep personal relationships tied these men together over decades, as friends,
family, and colleagues. For these viewers, the information provided by the annual
fasti would be more easily understood because it was already known: they might
note the cohort of magistrates in any given year and recall its events, holidays that
offered respite from daily life and banquets that solidified their bonds with their
peers through shared food and drink. For them, the calendar was a symbol of a
wider belief system, but it was an iteration of the calendar tailored to their own
society, which included a special day for them alone, the vernarum dies festus.
     This approach, I have argued, better reflects the agency of the men who ran and
oversaw the society that commissioned the calendar, treating the manner in which
their names were presented as deliberate choices and recognizing that they used the
inscription as a means of exercising the authority bestowed on them by their peers,
or purchased at considerable expense for men of their rank. To serve the society
by assuming a magistracy gave an individual a powerful voice in collective deci-
sion-making and provided a platform for recognition. The interventions evident on
the inscription, both additions and erasures, reveal the importance of communal
self-governance through formal process, including issuing decrees, implementing
them, and keeping records of such decisions, past and present. Moreover, leader-
ship roles were not reserved for those with high-status jobs. The hierarchy of the
voluntary association did not mirror that of the household. More often than not, the
leaders of the society came from its populous lower echelons, occupied by men
who performed manual labor and possessed little formal education. When they had
the honor of serving their community as leaders, they wielded social authority—
including the ability to offer special recognition or strip it away—over those who
determined the rules and organization of their day-to-day working lives. The nature
of the document, subject to different evidentiary biases than funerary evidence, also
shows that these individuals had different priorities for expressing status in a lived
context than in memorials. They frequently omitted markers of status that indicated
their relationship to the emperor (servus Caesaris, libertus Augusti), and empha-
sized job titles, in so doing affirming their ties to and place within a community
of peers for whom their work identities would be more significant than their legal
standing. A job that did not confer much prestige for the purposes of commem-
oration was here an important badge of inclusion in an association of those who
worked, and likely lived, together.
